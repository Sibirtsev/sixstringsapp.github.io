Parallel Keys: Major and Minor with the Same Tonic

Parallel keys share the same tonic but use different modes: C major and C minor both center on C, yet their third, sixth, and seventh scale degrees differ.

July 30, 2026
Reader level: Arranger

C major and C minor begin on the same note. Their tonic is C. But their scales, chords, and overall sound are different.

This relationship is called parallel keys. Understanding it helps explain how music can switch between major and minor without changing its tonal center, where borrowed chords come from, and why moving from major to minor can sound dramatic even when the tonic stays the same.

What are parallel keys?

Parallel keys are major and minor keys that share the same tonic.

Examples:

C major and C minor
D major and D minor
E major and E minor

Each pair shares the same first scale degree:

C major → tonic C
C minor → tonic C

The remaining scale degrees form different scales.

C major and C minor: the same tonic, different notes

Compare the C major and C natural minor scales:

C major:         C–D–E–F–G–A–B
C natural minor: C–D–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭

Four scale degrees remain unchanged:

1, 2, 4, 5
C, D, F, G

Three scale degrees are lowered in natural minor:

3 → ♭3
6 → ♭6
7 → ♭7

Their formulas are therefore:

major:         1–2–3–4–5–6–7
natural minor: 1–2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7
Scale degreeC majorC natural minorChange
1CCsame tonic
2DDunchanged
3EE♭lowered by a semitone
4FFunchanged
5GGunchanged
6AA♭lowered by a semitone
7BB♭lowered by a semitone

You hear C major first, followed by C natural minor. The tonic stays the same, but the lowered third, sixth, and seventh change the character of the key.

Why the third matters most

The third scale degree determines the quality of the tonic triad.

In C major:

C = C–E–G
1–3–5

In C minor:

Cm = C–E♭–G
1–♭3–5

Only one note changes: E moves down to E♭. That single change turns a major triad into a minor triad.

The third is the quickest aural clue that the mode has changed. The sixth and seventh reveal the wider difference in melodies, scales, and chord progressions.

How the chords change

Harmonizing both scales in triads produces two different sets of chords.

Scale degreeC majorC natural minor
I / iCCm
iiDmDdim
iii / IIIEmE♭
IV / ivFFm
V / vGGm
vi / VIAmA♭
vii° / VIIBdimB♭

In Roman-numeral analysis, major chords use uppercase numerals and minor chords use lowercase numerals. The tonic chord in a minor key is therefore written as i, not I.

The basic patterns are:

C major: I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii°
C minor: i–ii°–III–iv–v–VI–VII

In practical minor-key harmony, the fifth-degree chord is often made major:

Gm → G or G7
v  → V or V7

This requires raising B♭ to B. The raised note becomes a leading tone with a stronger pull toward C. A G or G7 chord in C minor therefore does not cancel the minor key.

Parallel and relative keys are not the same

These two relationships are easy to confuse.

RelationshipExampleWhat they shareWhat differs

relative keysC major and A minornotes and key signaturetonic

C major and A minor use the same notes:

C–D–E–F–G–A–B

But they have different tonal centers:

C major → C
A minor → A

C major and C minor share the same center, but their scales are different.

There is also a translation trap between English and Russian:

English “parallel keys” = Russian «одноимённые тональности»
English “relative keys” = Russian «параллельные тональности»

The English term parallel keys therefore should not be translated literally into Russian.

Their key signatures are different

Parallel keys do not normally share a key signature.

C major → no sharps or flats
C minor → B♭, E♭, A♭

These three flats correspond to the lowered third, sixth, and seventh degrees of the natural minor scale.

Individual notes can still be altered within minor-key music. In C minor, for example, B appears in the progression G7 → Cm. The key signature does not change; the raised note is written with an accidental.

How parallel keys are used

Changing the mode of an entire section

A verse may be in C major while the next section moves to C minor. The tonal center remains C, but the harmonic material changes.

Compare these two cadences:

C major: C–F–G–C
         I–IV–V–I

C minor: Cm–Fm–G–Cm
         i–iv–V–i

The minor-key version uses a major dominant chord, G. It contains the note B, which leads strongly back to the C minor tonic, Cm.

Borrowing individual chords

You do not need to move an entire section into minor. A major key can borrow a single chord from its parallel minor.

A common example is:

C–F–Fm–C
I–IV–iv–I

The Fm chord does not belong to C major. It comes from C minor. Only one note changes inside the chord:

F  = F–A–C
Fm = F–A♭–C

The motion A → A♭ → G creates a smooth chromatic pull toward the tonic.

Other chords commonly borrowed from the parallel minor are:

♭III, iv, ♭VI, ♭VII

In C major, these are:

E♭, Fm, A♭, B♭

Borrowed chords deserve a separate article. For now, the important point is their source: they come from the parallel minor.

How to hear the relationship between parallel keys

Do not rely only on mood. Major does not always sound happy, and minor does not always sound sad. Listen for specific scale degrees.

Find the shared tonic first

Play or sustain C as a drone, then play both scales. Both resolve toward C, even though the movement inside each scale is different.

Compare the thirds

Play:

C–E
C–E♭

The major third C–E points to major. The minor third C–E♭ points to minor.

Add the sixth and seventh

Compare:

A and A♭
B and B♭

These notes reveal more than the quality of the tonic chord: they help define the character of the entire scale.

How to explore parallel keys in Six Strings App

In Harmony, choose C, then compare C major and C minor. Look at:

  • the notes of each scale;
  • the diatonic chords;
  • the positions of the third, sixth, and seventh degrees;
  • major- and minor-key cadences.

In the Circle of Fifths, compare the key signatures of parallel keys. Sharing a tonic does not mean that two keys sit next to each other or use the same key signature.

In the Fretboard Explorer, open C major and C natural minor in interval mode. The positions of 1, 2, 4, and 5 remain the same, while 3, 6, and 7 move down by one fret. In practice mode, you can also turn on a drone and play both scales over the same tonal center.

A seven-minute exercise

1. Play the shared tonic

Find several C notes across the fretboard. Play them in different octaves and keep the tonal center in your ear.

2. Compare the scales

Play:

C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C
C–D–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭–C

In the second scale, focus on E♭, A♭, and B♭.

3. Compare the tonic chords

Play C, then Cm. Listen closely to the movement E → E♭.

4. Compare the cadences

C–F–G–C
Cm–Fm–G–Cm

Notice what remains the same and what changes.

5. Add a borrowed chord

Play:

C–F–Fm–C

Follow the upper or inner voice:

A → A♭ → G

6. Transfer the principle to another tonic

Compare A major and A natural minor:

A major:         A–B–C♯–D–E–F♯–G♯
A natural minor: A–B–C–D–E–F–G

Find the lowered ♭3, ♭6, and ♭7.

Common mistakes

Confusing parallel keys with relative keys. Parallel keys share one tonic. Relative keys share the same notes but have different tonics.

Assuming parallel keys contain the same notes. Only the tonal center is shared. In the basic comparison with natural minor, the third, sixth, and seventh degrees change.

Comparing major only with harmonic minor. Begin by comparing major with natural minor. The raised seventh in harmonic minor is an additional layer of minor-key harmony.

Assuming major is always happy and minor is always sad. Mode affects musical color, but tempo, rhythm, register, melody, dynamics, and timbre also shape the result.

Treating every minor chord as a modulation to minor. A single borrowed chord can change the color without changing the tonal center of the entire section.

Mixing up the English terminology. Parallel keys share a tonic. Relative keys share a key signature and pitch collection.

Treating the major dominant as a mistake in minor. The V and V7 chords come from the raised seventh degree and are commonly used to create a strong resolution to the minor tonic.

Summary

Parallel keys share a tonic but use different modes.

In the basic comparison between major and natural minor:

1, 2, 4, and 5 remain unchanged
3, 6, and 7 are lowered

C major and C minor are therefore both centered on C, but they use different scales and chords. This relationship explains both a direct change from major to minor and the origin of many borrowed chords.
