What Is an Arpeggio in Music

An arpeggio is a chord whose notes are played one after another instead of all at once. It turns a chord into motion: you can hear its notes separately, use it in a picking pattern, or build a melodic part around it.

May 30, 2026
Reader level: Musician

Strum a chord and its notes blend into one sound. Play the same chord tones one after another, and you hear a line that can move up, move down, or alternate between notes. That line is called an arpeggio.

Arpeggios are useful for more than soft song accompaniment. They help you hear what a chord is made of, find its notes on the fretboard, and choose notes to highlight in a melody or improvisation.

What Is an Arpeggio

An arpeggio is a chord whose notes are played one after another rather than at the same time.

Take a C major chord. It contains three different notes:

C  E  G
1  3  5

If these notes sound together, you hear a chord:

C + E + G

If the same notes sound one after another, you hear an arpeggio:

C → E → G → E

The notes have not changed. The way they are played has changed: a single block of harmony has become a pattern in time.

How Is an Arpeggio Different from a Chord?

A chord and an arpeggio can contain exactly the same notes. The difference is when those notes are heard.

What you playC major exampleWhat you hear
ChordC + E + Gone combined sound
ArpeggioC → E → G → Eseparate notes from the same chord

A chord immediately gives you the overall character of the harmony. An arpeggio opens that chord up: you can hear its root, third, and fifth separately.

This is especially useful on guitar. It is easy to memorise a chord shape mechanically. When you play its arpeggio and name the notes or intervals, the shape stops being just a diagram on the fretboard.

An Arpeggio Does Not Have to Move Upward

In the simplest example, an arpeggio moves from a lower note to a higher one:

C → E → G

But that is not a rule. You can play the chord tones in another order, repeat them, or place them in different octaves:

C → G → E
G → E → C
C → E → G → C → G → E

Every example above still uses the notes of C major: C, E, and G. They are all arpeggios of the same chord.

With an open C major chord, a guitarist could play this pattern:

String:   5  4  3  2  1  2  3  4
Note:     C  E  G  C  E  C  G  E
Degree:   1  3  5  1  3  1  5  3

There are more played notes because C and E appear again in higher octaves. The chord itself still consists of C, E, and G.

An Arpeggio and a Picking Pattern Are Not Quite the Same Thing

On guitar, arpeggios are often played with a picking pattern. The two ideas are connected, but they are not identical.

A picking pattern describes the movement of your picking hand: the order in which you pluck the strings.

An arpeggio describes the musical material: whether the notes in that pattern belong to a particular chord.

For example, if you hold a C major shape and pick only notes C, E, and G, you are playing a C major arpeggio. Add notes that are not part of that chord, and the result becomes a melodic line or an embellished accompaniment, not just a C major arpeggio.

This distinction matters when you write a guitar part: your hand may repeat the same picking motion while the notes underneath it change the harmony.

An Arpeggio Is Not a Scale Either

A scale gives you the notes of a musical mode or key. An arpeggio selects only the notes of a specific chord from that wider material.

For example, the C major scale contains seven different notes:

C major scale:       C  D  E  F  G  A  B
C major arpeggio:    C     E     G

When you play a scale, the melody often moves through neighbouring scale degrees. When you play an arpeggio, it jumps between the stable chord tones.

That is why an arpeggio usually outlines the current harmony more clearly. Over a C major chord, the notes C, E, and G sound like its foundation because they are already part of the chord.

The Arpeggio Changes with the Chord Type

Every chord has its own arpeggio. Change the chord tones, and the arpeggio changes with them.

ChordChord tonesArpeggio
C majorC, E, GC → E → G
C minorC, E♭, GC → E♭ → G
Cmaj7C, E, G, BC → E → G → B

In C minor, the third has changed: E becomes E♭. In Cmaj7, the note B has been added as the seventh. You can change the order of the notes, but an arpeggio still needs to preserve the tones of the chord you are playing.

Why Do Musicians Practise Arpeggios?

To Hear the Notes Inside a Chord

When a chord sounds as a whole, a beginner may find it difficult to distinguish its individual notes. In an arpeggio, each note is heard separately. This makes it easier to recognise the root, third, and fifth.

To Play Softer Accompaniment

A strummed chord gives a dense sound. An arpeggio leaves more space and creates movement. This often works well in quiet verses, intros, and parts where the voice should remain in front.

To Build Melodies and Solos Around the Harmony

When your phrase lands on notes from the chord that is playing, it usually sounds connected to the harmony. Arpeggios help you find those anchor notes deliberately instead of guessing.

To See the Fretboard More Clearly

A chord shape gives you one convenient fingering. An arpeggio shows where the notes of that chord lie around the shape and across the fretboard. This helps connect chords, melody, and improvisation.

How to Hear an Arpeggio

Take a familiar chord such as C or Am.

  1. First, strum it once downward. Listen to the combined chord sound.
  2. Then play its strings one at a time, slowly, without changing the shape.
  3. Compare the result: the harmony is still there, but now you can hear individual notes moving.
  4. Repeat the pattern in reverse.
  5. Try to sing the highest note in the pattern.

The main clue is this: in an arpeggio, you hear separate steps, but they still belong to one chord.

How to See an Arpeggio in Six Strings App

To explore the idea on the fretboard, open Chords, Arpeggios, and Scales.

  1. Select C as the root and major as the chord type.
  2. First, look at the chord tones: C, E, and G.
  3. Switch to the arpeggio view.
  4. Change the display from note names to intervals: 1, 3, and 5.
  5. Find these notes around a familiar C chord shape.
  6. Play several versions: upward, downward, and with the top note repeated.

Do not try to cover the whole fretboard at once. For a first practice session, one comfortable area and one chord are enough.

5-Minute Exercise

Take an open C chord.

  1. Play the full chord and name it: C.
  2. Play one string at a time: fifth, fourth, third, second, first.
  3. Name the notes you hear: C, E, G, C, E.
  4. Play the pattern in reverse: E, C, G, E, C.
  5. In Chords, Arpeggios, and Scales, turn on the interval display and play the same pattern as 1, 3, 5, 1, 3.
  6. Choose Am and repeat the process with the new chord.

Main goal: hear that an arpeggio is not a random sequence of plucked strings. It is the notes of a particular chord spread across time.

Common Mistakes

An arpeggio is not just any sequence of notes. If the notes do not belong to the chosen chord, the result may be a melody or part of a scale, but it is not a pure arpeggio of that chord.

An arpeggio does not have to move from low to high. You can play downward, alternate notes, or repeat them.

Repeating a note does not create a new chord. In open guitar shapes, the same notes often occur in different octaves.

A picking pattern does not automatically create an arpeggio. A picking pattern is the order in which you play the strings. It becomes an arpeggio when those strings produce the notes of the chosen chord.

An arpeggio does not replace a scale. A scale gives you a wider set of notes; an arpeggio outlines a specific chord.

In short

An arpeggio is a chord whose notes are played one after another. For C major, these notes can be C, E, and G in any useful order, with repeats in different octaves.

A chord gives you the overall harmony. An arpeggio helps you hear and play its inner structure. For guitarists, it is a step from memorising a shape to understanding notes, intervals, and harmony on the fretboard.
