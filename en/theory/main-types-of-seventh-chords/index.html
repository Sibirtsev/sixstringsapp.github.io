Main Types of Seventh Chords

A seventh chord is not one specific chord, but a whole family of chords. They share the same idea: a triad plus a seventh. But the triad, the seventh, and the fifth can differ, so Cmaj7, C7, Cm7, Cm7♭5, and Cdim7 all sound different.

June 15, 2026
Reader level: Arranger

In the article What Is a Seventh Chord, we looked at the basic idea: a seventh chord is a triad with an added seventh.

But that is not enough to read chord symbols confidently.

Cmaj7, C7, Cm7, Cm7♭5, and Cdim7 are all seventh chords. Each one has four notes. But they are different chords: they have different formulas, different sounds, and different roles in harmony.

This article is a reference guide to the main types of seventh chords. The goal is not just to memorize names, but to understand which intervals each chord contains.

Main idea

The type of seventh chord depends on three things:

which third
which fifth
which seventh

Or, even simpler:

triad + type of seventh = type of seventh chord

For example:

Cmaj7 = major triad + major seventh
C7    = major triad + minor seventh
Cm7   = minor triad + minor seventh
Cm7♭5 = diminished triad + minor seventh
Cdim7 = diminished triad + diminished seventh

The number 7 in a chord name only tells you that the chord contains a seventh. But the seventh itself may be major, minor, or diminished. That is why chords with 7 in the name do not all sound the same.

A formula is more useful than a random fingering. It shows which scale degrees create the character of the chord.

On guitar, the same seventh chord may have many shapes. But the formula stays the same.

How to read seventh chord formulas

A formula is written relative to the root of the chord.

For a C chord, the root is C:

C = 1

Then we count scale degrees from C:

C D E F G A B
1 2 3 4 5 6 7

If the formula says 3, we take E. If it says ♭3, we lower E to E♭.

If it says 5, we take G. If it says ♭5, we lower G to G♭.

The seventh works the same way:

7   = B
♭7  = B♭
𝄫7  = B𝄫

B𝄫 means B double-flat. On guitar and piano, it sounds like A, but in the formula of Cdim7 it is B𝄫 because it is the diminished seventh above C.

This may look unusual at first, but the spelling helps preserve the logic of the chord.

Cmaj7: major seventh chord

Cmaj7 is built from a major triad and a major seventh.

Formula:

1–3–5–7

Notes:

C–E–G–B

Cmaj7 sounds soft, wide, and slightly dreamy. It has a major foundation, but the major seventh makes the sound more delicate and less direct.

Compare:

C      = C–E–G
Cmaj7  = C–E–G–B

A regular C chord sounds simpler and more stable. Cmaj7 sounds richer, but not necessarily tense. It adds a bright color.

This chord often appears in jazz, soul, neo-soul, pop music, lo-fi, and calmer harmonic contexts.

C7: dominant seventh chord

C7 is built from a major triad and a minor seventh.

Formula:

1–3–5–♭7

Notes:

C–E–G–B♭

C7 sounds more active and more tense than Cmaj7. It often wants to resolve to F:

C7 → F

The reason is in two notes inside C7:

E  = third
B♭ = minor seventh

They pull toward notes of the F chord:

E  → F
B♭ → A

So C7 is not just “C with an added note”. It is a chord with direction.

In harmony, this type is called a dominant seventh chord. It is especially important on the V degree of a key.

Cm7: minor seventh chord

Cm7 is built from a minor triad and a minor seventh.

Formula:

1–♭3–5–♭7

Notes:

C–E♭–G–B♭

Cm7 sounds soft, minor, and denser than a regular minor triad.

Compare:

Cm   = C–E♭–G
Cm7  = C–E♭–G–B♭

A regular Cm chord gives the basic minor color. Cm7 makes that color deeper and calmer.

Cm7 does not demand resolution as strongly as C7. It is often used as a soft minor chord in jazz, blues, funk, soul, pop music, and rock.

Cm7♭5: half-diminished seventh chord

Cm7♭5 is built from a diminished triad and a minor seventh.

Formula:

1–♭3–♭5–♭7

Notes:

C–E♭–G♭–B♭

This chord is called half-diminished because it contains a diminished triad:

C–E♭–G♭

But its seventh is minor:

B♭ = ♭7

You may see two common symbols:

Cm7♭5
Cø

The symbol ø means half-diminished.

Cm7♭5 sounds unstable and dark. It is often used as a preparatory chord, especially in minor harmony and jazz progressions.

Cdim7: diminished seventh chord

Cdim7 is built from a diminished triad and a diminished seventh.

Formula:

1–♭3–♭5–𝄫7

Notes:

C–E♭–G♭–B𝄫

On the instrument, B𝄫 sounds like A, so in practical examples you may sometimes see this spelling:

C–E♭–G♭–A

But theoretically, for Cdim7 it is more correct to write:

C–E♭–G♭–B𝄫

This preserves the formula: root, third, fifth, and seventh.

Cdim7 sounds very tense and compressed. It is easy to recognize by its symmetrical structure: it is made of stacked minor thirds.

C → E♭  = minor third
E♭ → G♭ = minor third
G♭ → B𝄫 = minor third

In semitones:

C → E♭ → G♭ → B𝄫
  3    3    3

Because of this symmetry, a diminished seventh chord is easy to move in minor thirds. On guitar, this often gives the same shapes every three frets.

Half-diminished and diminished: what is the difference?

Cm7♭5 and Cdim7 are similar because both are based on a diminished triad:

C–E♭–G♭

The difference is the seventh:

Cm7♭5 = C–E♭–G♭–B♭
Cdim7 = C–E♭–G♭–B𝄫

In other words:

Cm7♭5: ♭7
Cdim7: 𝄫7

The half-diminished chord sounds unstable, but softer. The diminished seventh chord sounds sharper and more symmetrical.

The main mistake is to treat ø and °7 as the same thing. They are different symbols and different formulas.

How seventh chords work in a key

In a major key, seventh chords can be built on every scale degree.

In C major, we get:

Imaj7   = Cmaj7
iim7    = Dm7
iiim7   = Em7
IVmaj7  = Fmaj7
V7      = G7
vim7    = Am7
viim7♭5 = Bm7♭5

As a sequence:

Cmaj7 – Dm7 – Em7 – Fmaj7 – G7 – Am7 – Bm7♭5

G7 is especially important here. It is the dominant seventh chord on the V degree. It pulls toward C:

G7 → C

In minor and chromatic harmony, half-diminished and diminished seventh chords appear more often. They help intensify motion toward a stable chord.

How to memorize seventh chords

Do not start with dozens of fingerings. Start with formulas.

For every seventh chord, ask three questions:

which third: 3 or ♭3?
which fifth: 5 or ♭5?
which seventh: 7, ♭7, or 𝄫7?

Example:

C7
third: 3
fifth: 5
seventh: ♭7
formula: 1–3–5–♭7

Another example:

Cm7♭5
third: ♭3
fifth: ♭5
seventh: ♭7
formula: 1–♭3–♭5–♭7

This turns the chord from a random group of letters and numbers into a clear internal structure.

How to see this on the fretboard

On guitar, it is important to see not only the shape, but also the intervals.

In every seventh chord, find:

1 = root
3 or ♭3 = third
5 or ♭5 = fifth
7, ♭7, or 𝄫7 = seventh

The third and seventh are especially important. They often define the character and function of the chord.

For example, in C7:

E  = 3
B♭ = ♭7

If you play only E and B♭, the chord is already recognizable as a dominant sound. The root and fifth add fullness, but in an ensemble they are not always required.

5-minute exercise

  1. Take the root C.
  2. Play Cmaj7: C–E–G–B.
  3. Play C7: C–E–G–B♭.
  4. Play Cm7: C–E♭–G–B♭.
  5. Play Cm7♭5: C–E♭–G♭–B♭.
  6. Play Cdim7: C–E♭–G♭–B𝄫.
  7. After each chord, pause and describe the feeling.

Do not rush. The main goal is to hear the character: soft, tense, minor, unstable, or compressed.

Resolution exercise

Play three movements:

C7 → F
G7 → C
Bdim7 → C

Listen to how tension moves into support. Pay special attention to semitone motion:

B → C
F → E

These movements help explain why seventh chords are so important in harmony.

Guitar exercise

  1. Choose C7.
  2. Find C, E, G, and B♭ on the fretboard.
  3. Play the full shape.
  4. Then play only E and B♭.
  5. Add C.
  6. Add G.
  7. Compare when the chord starts to sound like C7.

This trains not only your fingers, but also your understanding of each note’s function inside the chord.

Common mistakes

Thinking that all chords with the number 7 are similar. Cmaj7, C7, Cm7, Cm7♭5, and Cdim7 are different chords. They have different formulas, different sounds, and different roles.

Confusing maj7 and 7. Cmaj7 contains the major seventh B. C7 contains the minor seventh B♭. A one-semitone difference completely changes the character of the chord.

Confusing ø and °7. Cø usually means Cm7♭5: a half-diminished seventh chord. C°7 means Cdim7: a diminished seventh chord.

Ignoring the double-flat in dim7. In Cdim7, the last note is B𝄫, not simply A. On the instrument it is the same pitch, but in the formula it is the diminished seventh.

Memorizing only the fingering. A shape is useful, but without intervals it is easy to forget. It is better to understand where the third, fifth, and seventh are.

Always playing all four notes. In an ensemble, the third and seventh are sometimes enough. The root may be played by the bass, and the fifth can often be omitted if it is not altered.

Short summary

Seventh chords differ not only because they contain a seventh. What matters is which triad forms the foundation, which seventh is added, and whether the fifth is altered. Cmaj7, C7, Cm7, Cm7♭5, and Cdim7 sound different because they have different formulas. The better you see intervals, the easier it becomes to read chord symbols, find shapes on the fretboard, and hear the function of the chord.
