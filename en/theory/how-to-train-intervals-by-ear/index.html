How to Train Intervals by Ear

Training intervals by ear means learning to recognise the distance between two sounds. It helps you work out melodies, sing more accurately, understand chords, and connect what you hear with what you see on the fretboard.

Reader level: Musician

How to Train Intervals by Ear

An interval is the distance between two sounds. When you hear one note move slightly upward, jump sharply higher, or combine with another note to create tension, you are already hearing intervals.

Interval training is not about guessing names quickly. It is about hearing movement and combinations of sounds. This makes it easier to work out melodies, analyse parts, sing the right notes, and understand what chords are made of.

Training intervals by ear means listening to the distance between two sounds, naming it, repeating it with your voice, and finding it on your instrument.

Keep practice short and regular:

  1. First, recognise direction: upward, downward, or the same note repeated.
  2. Practise small groups of intervals, not all of them at once.
  3. Listen to melodic and harmonic intervals separately.
  4. Repeat intervals with your voice.
  5. Check them on the fretboard.
  6. Gradually add new intervals and new keys.

The goal is to connect four things: sound, name, voice, and position on the instrument.

Melodic and Harmonic Intervals

There are two main types of intervals.

A melodic interval means two notes sound one after another:

C → E

This interval feels like movement. It matters in melodies, vocal lines, riffs, and solos.

A harmonic interval means two notes sound at the same time:

C + E

This interval feels like a combination of sounds. It matters in chords, double-stops, voice leading, and the feeling of stability or tension.

You need both types. If you practise only melodic intervals, you may hear movement better but struggle with sonorities. If you practise only harmonic intervals, you may hear combinations better but feel melodic direction less clearly.

What to Listen for in an Interval

Direction

For a melodic interval, first notice where the second note goes:

  • above the first note;
  • below the first note;
  • stays at the same pitch.

If you are not sure about the direction yet, do not rush to guess “third” or “fifth.” First learn to hear upward and downward motion clearly.

Distance

After direction, listen to the size of the step or leap.

C → D  small step
C → G  wide leap

At first, it is enough to hear “close” and “wide.” Later you can name the interval more precisely: second, third, fourth, fifth.

Character

An interval also has a character. Some intervals sound calm; others sound tense. Some feel close; others feel wide. Some can stay as they are; others seem to ask for resolution.

For example:

C + D  close and tense combination
C + G  wider and more stable combination

You do not need poetic descriptions. Use simple, honest words: “close,” “wide,” “calm,” “tense,” “wants to resolve.”

Why You Should Not Learn Intervals Only Through Songs

A common method is to remember each interval by the beginning of a familiar song. One song helps you remember a fourth, another helps you remember a sixth.

This can help at the beginning, but it has a limit: you may start recalling the song cue instead of hearing the interval itself.

Use songs as an extra reference, but build your main practice differently:

  • listen to the interval many times;
  • sing it with your voice;
  • find it on your instrument;
  • compare it with neighbouring intervals;
  • try it from different notes.

This way your ear remembers the distance between sounds, not just one familiar melody.

Which Intervals to Start With

Do not try to practise every interval right away. Start with small groups.

This group helps you hear distances that often appear in chords, riffs, and bass lines.

Minor and Major Intervals

When the general distances become clearer, add interval quality:

C → D♭  minor second
C → D   major second
C → E♭  minor third
C → E   major third

Do not start here if you still confuse direction and the general size of the leap. Minor and major intervals require more precise hearing.

How to Practise Without Guessing

The main mistake is naming an answer at random. Before answering, make a quick check.

For a melodic interval:

  1. Is the second note higher or lower?
  2. Is the distance small, medium, or wide?
  3. Can I repeat this movement with my voice?
  4. Where is a similar movement on the fretboard?

For a harmonic interval:

  1. Do the notes sound close or wide?
  2. Is the combination calm or tense?
  3. Can I pick out the upper note with my voice?
  4. Which two notes could create this sound on the instrument?

Even if the answer is wrong, this check is useful. You are not guessing; you are learning to describe what you hear.

Why Singing Intervals Helps

Singing quickly shows how well the interval is internalised. You may recognise the answer in an exercise but still be unable to repeat the distance with your voice. That means the connection is still weak.

You do not need a beautiful voice. Quietly repeat the movement on any comfortable syllable.

Example:

  1. Play C.
  2. Play C → E.
  3. Sing the movement on “la.”
  4. Check yourself on the instrument.
  5. Repeat C → G.

The voice connects your ear and body. For a musician, this matters more than knowing interval names in theory.

How to Connect Intervals with the Fretboard

For guitarists, an interval should not only be heard. It should also be found on the fretboard.

Take C on the 5th string, 3rd fret. Intervals are especially clear on one string: each fret raises the pitch by one semitone.

C → D   major second  +2 frets
C → E   major third   +4 frets
C → F   fourth        +5 frets
C → G   fifth         +7 frets

Play these movements slowly. At first, look at the frets. Then try to hear the next note before you play it.

This turns intervals from abstract theory into something practical. You hear the distance and immediately see how it works on the instrument.

7-Minute Exercise

2 Minutes: Direction

Play pairs of notes one after another and answer only one question: is the second note higher, lower, or the same?

C → D
C → B
C → C

Do not name the intervals yet. First, control the direction.

2 Minutes: A Small Group of Intervals

Use only three options:

unison
second
third

After each example, say a simple description: “repeat,” “small step,” or “medium leap.”

2 Minutes: Voice

Choose one interval, for example C → E.

  1. Play it.
  2. Sing it.
  3. Check it on the instrument.
  4. Repeat from another note.

1 Minute: Fretboard

Find the same intervals on one string:

C → D
C → E
C → G

Play them as melodic movements. Then try to play the two notes together, or very quickly one after another, to feel the harmonic sound.

Main goal: connect the interval name, melodic movement, harmonic sound, voice, and fretboard position.

Common Mistakes

Practising all intervals at once. This quickly turns practice into guessing. Start with 2–3 intervals and expand the set gradually.

Relying only on song cues. Songs can help, but your ear needs to recognise the distance between sounds, not remember a melody password.

Not repeating the correct answer. After a mistake, listen to the correct interval again and repeat it with your voice.

Not connecting the ear to the instrument. If you can hear an interval but cannot find it on the fretboard, the skill is incomplete.

Practising for too long. The ear gets tired quickly. Five to ten minutes every day is better than one long session once a week.

In short

Interval training is the practice of recognising the distance between sounds.

Melodic intervals sound one after another and help you hear melodic movement. Harmonic intervals sound at the same time and help you hear combinations, stability, and tension.

Start with direction and small groups. Sing intervals, check them on the fretboard, and gradually add new options. This turns intervals into a practical ear skill for melodies, chords, song learning, and improvisation.
