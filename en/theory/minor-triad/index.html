Minor Triad: The 1–♭3–5 Formula

A minor triad is a basic minor chord made of three notes: the root, the minor third, and the perfect fifth. It differs from a major triad by only one note, but that one note changes the whole character of the chord.

June 8, 2026
Reader level: Musician

Minor triads appear in songs just as often as major triads. Chords like Am, Dm, Em, Cm, and Gm are all minor triads if they do not contain any extra chord tones.

The main idea is simple: a minor triad is almost the same as a major triad, but its third is one semitone lower. That minor third is what creates the minor quality.

Simple definition

A minor triad is a chord made of three notes:

  1. the root;
  2. the minor third;
  3. the perfect fifth.

The short formula is:

1–♭3–5

Here 1 is the root of the chord, ♭3 is the minor third above the root, and 5 is the perfect fifth.

For example, C minor contains the notes C, E♭, and G:

C — 1
E♭ — ♭3
G — 5

What makes the triad minor

The minor quality comes from the minor third.

In C minor, the note E♭ is three semitones above C:

C → D♭ → D → E♭

That interval is a minor third. It is one semitone lower than a major third.

Compare the two chords:

C major = C–E–G
C minor = C–E♭–G

Only one note changes: E becomes E♭. But the character of the chord changes a lot.

Try not to hear this only as “happy versus sad”. A more useful idea is this: in major, the third is higher; in minor, the third is lower. That difference gives the chord a different character.

The formula in semitones

A minor triad can be counted from the root.

From the root to the minor third — 3 semitones. From the root to the perfect fifth — 7 semitones.

1 → ♭3 = 3 semitones
1 → 5  = 7 semitones

For C minor:

C → E♭ = 3 semitones
C → G  = 7 semitones

This method helps you build minor chords from any note. Choose a root, count 3 semitones to find the minor third, and count 7 semitones to find the perfect fifth.

Examples of minor triads from different roots

The 1–♭3–5 formula works from any root. The starting note changes, but the logic stays the same: root, minor third, perfect fifth.

ChordFormulaNotes
A minor1–♭3–5A–C–E
D minor1–♭3–5D–F–A
E minor1–♭3–5E–G–B
C minor1–♭3–5C–E♭–G
G minor1–♭3–5G–B♭–D

Notice C minor and G minor. They contain flats: E♭ and B♭. This is normal. The formula requires a minor third, so sometimes the note has to be flat.

For example, G minor is built from G, B♭, and D. If you use B instead of B♭, the chord is no longer minor. It becomes a major triad:

G minor = G–B♭–D
G major = G–B–D

The root and the fifth stay the same. The third changes — and the character of the chord changes with it.

Minor and major triads side by side

The easiest way to understand a minor triad is to compare it with a major triad.

Let’s use the same root: A.

A minor = A–C–E
A major = A–C#–E

Both chords contain A and E. Only the middle note changes:

C  = minor third
C# = major third

The third decides whether the triad is minor or major.

1–♭3–5 = minor triad
1–3–5  = major triad

So the point is not just to memorize “Am = A–C–E”. The point is to hear the role of the third. If the third is lower, the chord sounds minor. If the third is raised, the chord becomes major.

How to hear a minor triad

A minor triad often sounds softer, darker, or more tense than a major triad. But it is not useful to remember it only as a “sad” chord. Minor can sound calm, confident, aggressive, gentle, or dramatic.

For ear training, a more useful idea is this: a minor triad contains a minor third.

First, listen to C minor as a full chord:

Try to hear the three roles:

  • C — the center of the chord;
  • E♭ — the minor color;
  • G — stability and width.

Then play only C and E♭. The sound becomes thinner, but the minor color remains. This helps you hear the role of the minor third.

How to see a minor triad on the fretboard

On guitar, a minor chord may look like one familiar shape, but behind the shape there are always intervals.

For example, an open A minor shape contains the notes A, E, A, C, and E. Five strings are sounding, but there are only three note names:

A minor = A, C, E
Open A minor shape = A, E, A, C, E

Here:

A — 1
C — ♭3
E — 5

That is why a chord is not the same thing as a finger shape. A chord is a set of notes. A fingering is one way to play those notes on an instrument.

To check this in practice, open Chords, Arpeggios, Scales, choose A minor or C minor, and switch the display from notes to intervals. This makes it easier to see where 1, ♭3, and 5 are located in different fingerings.

One set of notes, different ways to play it

A minor triad can be played as a chord, as an arpeggio, or as part of a riff.

Take A minor:

A–C–E

If you play these notes together, you get a chord:

A + C + E = A minor

If you play them one after another, you get an arpeggio:

A → C → E → A

If you repeat the same notes in a rhythm, they can become a riff or a melodic phrase:

A → C → E → C

The musical material is the same: A, C, and E. Only the way of playing changes. This is why the 1–♭3–5 formula is useful beyond chord shapes. It also helps you find target notes for arpeggios, melodies, and improvisation.

For example, if the accompaniment is playing A minor, the notes A, C, and E will feel like stable target notes. They are good places to end phrases, add accents, or build short melodic answers.

Minor triad and arpeggio

If you play the notes of a minor triad together, you get a chord.

If you play them one by one, you get an arpeggio.

A + C + E together = A minor chord
A → C → E → A one by one = A minor arpeggio

An arpeggio helps you hear the minor chord from the inside. In improvisation, this is useful: if you land on A, C, and E over an Am chord, the phrase sounds connected to the harmony.

Why you should know the 1–♭3–5 formula

The minor triad formula helps you avoid memorizing every fingering as a separate object.

It helps you:

  • understand what a minor chord contains;
  • build minor chords from any root;
  • find minor arpeggios across the fretboard;
  • see which note creates the minor sound;
  • compare minor and major triads;
  • analyze unfamiliar fingerings;
  • choose notes more accurately for solos.

If you know that A minor is A, C, and E, it becomes easier to understand why changing C to C# turns A minor into A major. Only one note changes, but it changes the quality of the whole triad.

5-minute exercise

  1. Play an open A minor chord.
  2. Find the notes A, C, and E inside it.
  3. Play them one by one as an arpeggio: A → C → E → A.
  4. Play only A and C.
  5. Then play A and C#.
  6. Play A major: A → C# → E → A.
  7. Compare A minor and A major by ear.

The main goal is to hear that a minor triad is not tied to one shape. It is three notes that can be placed on the fretboard in different ways. And the minor quality depends mostly on the minor third.

Common mistakes

Thinking that minor always means sad. Minor often sounds darker than major, but mood depends on context. A fast minor riff can sound energetic, while a slow major chord can sound sad.

Looking for minor only through finger shapes. A shape helps you play, but it does not explain the chord. It is more reliable to look at the intervals: 1, ♭3, and 5.

Confusing the minor third with a lowered fifth. In a minor triad, the third is lowered. The fifth stays perfect. If you lower the fifth, you get a different type of chord.

Thinking that a triad must always use exactly three strings. On guitar, a minor triad often sounds on four, five, or six strings. That is normal: some notes are repeated in other octaves.

Calling any sad-sounding chord minor. Mood is not a reliable criterion. A minor triad is defined by its notes: root, minor third, and perfect fifth.

Short summary

A minor triad is built with the formula 1–♭3–5. Its main feature is the minor third. If a chord contains the root, minor third, and perfect fifth, it is a minor triad — no matter which fingering you use.
