Diminished and Augmented Triads

Besides major and minor triads, there are two important forms: diminished and augmented. They sound more tense and help create motion in harmony.

June 13, 2026
Reader level: Arranger

Major and minor triads sound relatively stable. They are easy to hear as points of support: major through the major third, minor through the minor third.

But harmony does not need only stable colors. Sometimes a chord has to pull forward, create a question, strengthen a transition, or briefly remove the feeling of “home”.

That is where diminished and augmented triads are useful. They are not a separate layer of complicated theory. They are still triads made of three notes: root, third, and fifth. The difference is which third and which fifth the chord contains.

Simple definition

A diminished triad is a triad with a minor third and a diminished fifth.

Formula:

1–♭3–♭5

An augmented triad is a triad with a major third and an augmented fifth.

Formula:

1–3–♯5

In simpler terms:

diminished = a minor triad with a lowered fifth

augmented = a major triad with a raised fifth

Four triad types side by side

Let’s compare the four main triads from C.

Triad typeFormulaNotes from CWhat changes
Major1–3–5C–E–Gmajor third and perfect fifth
Minor1–♭3–5C–E♭–Gthe third is lowered by a semitone
Diminished1–♭3–♭5C–E♭–G♭the third and fifth are lowered
Augmented1–3–♯5C–E–G♯the fifth is raised by a semitone

The character changes not because the chord has become “complex”. It changes because the interval structure changes.

Diminished triad: formula and sound

A diminished triad is built with this formula:

1–♭3–♭5

For example, C diminished:

C–E♭–G♭

Here:

C  = 1
E♭ = ♭3
G♭ = ♭5

This chord sounds compressed, tense, and unstable. The reason is the lowered fifth: instead of the usual point of support, the chord gets a sharper interval.

A diminished triad consists of two minor thirds stacked on top of each other:

C → E♭  = minor third
E♭ → G♭ = minor third

In semitones:

C → E♭ → G♭
  3st  3st

Here st means semitones.

Augmented triad: formula and sound

An augmented triad is built with this formula:

1–3–♯5

For example, C augmented:

C–E–G♯

Here:

C  = 1
E  = 3
G♯ = ♯5

This chord sounds expanded, strange, and suspended. It resembles a major triad, but instead of the calm perfect fifth, it contains a raised fifth.

An augmented triad consists of two major thirds stacked on top of each other:

C → E  = major third
E → G♯ = major third

In semitones:

C → E → G♯
  4st 4st

If we continue by one more major third, we arrive back at C in the next octave:

C → E → G♯ → C
  4   4    4

Because of this symmetry, an augmented triad sounds less stable: the ear does not always immediately understand where its center is.

How they differ from major and minor

Major and minor triads differ mainly by the third:

C major = C–E–G
C minor = C–E♭–G

In both cases, the fifth stays perfect:

G = 5

Diminished and augmented triads change the feeling of the fifth:

C diminished = C–E♭–G♭
C augmented  = C–E–G♯

In a diminished triad, the fifth contracts:

G → G♭

In an augmented triad, the fifth expands:

G → G♯

That is why it is useful to hear these chords as changes to familiar forms: the major and minor triads.

Where diminished triads appear

A diminished triad often appears where harmony wants to resolve.

On the seventh degree of major

In C major, the seventh scale degree gives us B diminished:

C major: C D E F G A B
seventh degree: B–D–F

In harmonic notation:

I    ii   iii  IV   V    vi   vii°
C    Dm   Em   F    G    Am   Bdim

The ° symbol often means a diminished chord:

B° = B diminished

B diminished pulls toward C major because B wants to move up to C, while F often wants to move down to E:

B → C
F → E

In harmonic minor

Diminished chords are also important in minor keys, especially when the raised seventh degree appears.

For example, A harmonic minor contains G♯. From that note, we can build G♯ diminished:

G♯–B–D

This chord strongly pulls toward A minor.

As a passing chord

A diminished triad can also work between two more stable chords:

C → C#dim → Dm

Here the diminished chord creates a chromatic bridge between C and Dm.

Where augmented triads appear

An augmented triad most often appears as a passing harmony or as a color chord.

Between I and IV

For example:

C → Caug → F

The top voice moves smoothly:

G → G♯ → A

The listener hears not just a chord change, but a small chromatic movement inside the harmony.

As a stronger dominant color

An augmented sound can also appear inside dominant harmony.

For example, G augmented can pull toward C:

G–B–D♯ → C–E–G

Here D♯ wants to rise to E, and B wants to resolve to C. This creates strong directed motion.

As an unusual color

Sometimes an augmented triad is used not for strict resolution, but for color: strange, magical, anxious, or suspended.

At first, it is better to study it through simple resolutions. That makes it easier to hear why it appears in harmony at all.

How these chords are written

A diminished triad may be written like this:

Cdim
C°
Cm♭5

For a triad, Cdim and C° are the most common options.

An augmented triad may be written like this:

Caug
C+
C(#5)

The most common spellings are Caug and C+.

Important: Cdim and Cdim7 are not the same thing. Cdim is the triad C–E♭–G♭. Cdim7 is a seventh chord with a fourth note.

Enharmonic spelling

Sometimes the same piano key or guitar fret can have different note names. For example, G♯ and A♭ sound the same on an equal-tempered instrument, but they can have different theoretical meanings.

For C augmented, it is better to write:

C–E–G♯

Not:

C–E–A♭

Why? Because the chord formula is 1–3–♯5. The fifth above C is G. The raised fifth is G♯.

For C diminished, it is better to write:

C–E♭–G♭

Not:

C–D♯–F♯

Why? Because the formula is 1–♭3–♭5. The third above C is E, so the lowered third is E♭. The fifth above C is G, so the lowered fifth is G♭.

On the instrument, these may look like the same keys or frets. In analysis, the spelling matters.

How to see this on the fretboard

On guitar, it is important to see not only the shape, but also the intervals.

In a diminished triad, look for:

1, ♭3, ♭5

In an augmented triad:

1, 3, ♯5

The ♭5 and ♯5 are what give these chords their character.

Open Chords, Arpeggios, Scales, choose a diminished or augmented triad, and turn on interval view. This makes it easier to see which note makes the chord diminished or augmented.

If you want to see these sounds across the whole fretboard, open the Fretboard Explorer and compare the perfect fifth, ♭5, and ♯5 from the same root.

5-minute exercise

  1. Play C major: C–E–G.
  2. Raise G to G♯. You get C augmented.
  3. Return to G and lower E to E♭. You get C minor.
  4. Now also lower G to G♭. You get C diminished.
  5. Compare the feeling of each form.
C major      = C–E–G
C augmented  = C–E–G♯
C minor      = C–E♭–G
C diminished = C–E♭–G♭

The main goal is to hear how one changed note can change the character of the chord.

Resolution exercise

  1. Play B diminished: B–D–F.
  2. After it, play C major: C–E–G.
  3. Listen to how B pulls toward C, while F pulls toward E.
  4. Repeat this several times.
  5. Then play C → Caug → F.
  6. Listen to the top-line movement: G → G♯ → A.

This helps you hear two different types of tension: compressed diminished tension and expanded augmented tension.

Common mistakes

Thinking that diminished and augmented chords are just “complex chords”. They are ordinary triads with an altered fifth. They contain only three notes.

Ignoring intervals. The chord name becomes clear only through 1, 3, 5 and their alterations: ♭3, ♭5, ♯5.

Confusing a diminished triad with a diminished seventh chord. A diminished triad has three notes. A diminished seventh chord has four.

Writing the notes randomly. C augmented is C–E–G♯, not C–E–A♭. C diminished is C–E♭–G♭, not C–D♯–F♯.

Using a tense chord without understanding its resolution. You can leave the tension open, but first it is useful to hear where the chord naturally wants to go.

Looking only for a fingering. The shape on the fretboard matters, but without intervals it is easy to forget.

Short summary

A diminished triad has the formula 1–♭3–♭5. An augmented triad has the formula 1–3–♯5. Both chords sound more tense than major and minor triads because their fifth is altered. Most often, they work not as points of rest, but as motion: toward a resolution, a transition, or a new harmonic color.
