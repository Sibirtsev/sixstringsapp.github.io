Microtone Trainer: How I Learned to Hear Guitar Bends and Intonation More Accurately

A guitar bend can feel technically correct and still miss its pitch. I built a microtone trainer to hear these small mistakes sooner — and bring that awareness back to the guitar.

Why I Started Training My Ear for Guitar Bends

For a long time, bends were one of those techniques where my hand roughly knew what to do, but my ear did not always tell me where the note had landed. I would push the string up and hear the familiar rise in pitch, yet miss that I had stopped a little below the target — or already pushed past it.

Half-step bends were especially difficult for me. The movement is small: the pitch only needs to reach the note one fret higher. It sounds as though that should be easier than a whole-step bend, but I found the opposite. With such a short movement, it is easy to overshoot the target, and even a small mistake makes the phrase sound uncertain.

Over time, I noticed another problem. Sometimes a note sounded slightly sharp even without a bend, simply because I was pressing the string too hard against the fretboard. The guitar could be in tune and the fingering could be right, but unnecessary pressure from my fretting hand still affected the intonation.

That is when I realised I needed to train more than my fingers. I needed to hear small changes in pitch more clearly.

Repeating Bends on the Guitar Was Not Enough

A bend is not only a physical movement. To play it accurately, you need to hear the target pitch and stop at the right moment.

You can repeat the same movement again and again: place a finger, push the string, release it, try once more. That helps with technique. But while your ear cannot distinguish a small pitch error, it is difficult to know what to fix. Did I stop below the target? Did I go too far? Or was the problem not the bend at all, but the pressure of my fretting hand?

I wanted to develop a skill that comes before judging whether a phrase sounds “good” or “bad”. First, I needed to hear one simple thing: did the second note move higher or lower than the first?

That is what microtonal hearing means in this practical guitar context: noticing small differences in pitch between very close sounds. Not as an abstract idea from experimental music, but in ordinary playing — when you need to fret a clean note, land a bend accurately, or avoid squeezing a string sharp.

I Wanted a Short Practice Exercise, Not a Test

Trainers for this kind of ear practice already exist. I looked for suitable options online, but the format often got in the way of the actual exercise.

Some tools did not let me choose a sound I was comfortable listening to. Others made the exercise feel like a long test: I had to answer many questions before I could start or see any result. That approach may be useful for measuring a skill, but it was not what I needed.

I wanted to open a page, hear two notes, answer, check myself immediately, and continue. A few minutes of practice, then back to the guitar.

I already had my own practice tools in Six Strings App. In one place, I could work on rhythm, explore chords and scales, or learn the fretboard. Adding one more small trainer felt natural: fewer switches between websites, less setup before starting, and a better chance that I would actually practise regularly.

How the Microtone Trainer Works

The exercise is intentionally direct.

The trainer plays one note, followed by a second note. The second pitch is slightly different: it is either higher or lower than the first. The task is to identify the direction of that change.

The correct answer appears immediately after each response. There is no need to finish a long sequence before seeing the result: listen, choose, check, and try again.

As the difficulty increases, the distance between the two pitches becomes smaller. At first, the difference is easier to notice. Later, it becomes subtler, and you need to listen more carefully to the direction of the pitch movement.

The trainer also lets you choose the sound timbre. This matters to me: ear training should not be made harder by a sound that is tiring or distracting. With a comfortable timbre, it is easier to focus on the pitch difference and return to the exercise regularly.

The pitch difference is measured in cents. For practice, the important point is simple: the fewer cents there are between two notes, the closer they sound and the harder it becomes to hear which one is higher.

Why the Trainer Does Not Replace the Guitar

The trainer cannot teach your fingers how to place a bend, how much force to apply, or how to control vibrato. It does not replace playing the real instrument.

Its purpose is different: it makes your ear more attentive to small pitch errors. Then you need to transfer that attention to the guitar.

That is how I used it. I spent a little time comparing notes in the trainer, then picked up the guitar and tested the same awareness with my hands.

For a half-step bend, the process can be very simple:

  1. Play the note where the bend begins.
  2. Play the target note one fret higher and remember its sound.
  3. Return to the starting fret and bend the string towards that pitch.
  4. Listen for whether the bend stopped below the target or went past it.
  5. Repeat slowly, without trying to play a full phrase at tempo straight away.

The same attention also became useful without bends. I started listening to ordinary fretted notes and noticing when excess finger pressure pushed them sharp. Before, I might only have felt that something sounded wrong. Now it became easier to understand why: the instrument was not always the issue; sometimes I was simply pressing the string too hard.

What Changed in My Playing

I cannot say that a few practice sessions automatically fixed all my bends. It does not work that way. Accuracy still requires time with the guitar: controlling the hand, the force of the movement, and the resulting sound.

But the most important thing changed: I started hearing mistakes sooner.

I notice more often when I overbend a note. I pay more attention to notes that go sharp because I press too hard. And with half-step bends, which used to frustrate me most, I now feel much more confident than before.

For me, that is the most useful result of ear training. It does not do the musician’s work. It helps you understand what is happening to the sound. Once you can hear the mistake, correcting it with your hands becomes much easier.

How to Try This Approach

Ear training does not have to become a separate, lengthy practice session. I prefer a short exercise that fits naturally into ordinary guitar practice.

Try this routine:

  1. Open the Microtone Trainer and choose a comfortable timbre.
  2. Spend a few minutes deciding whether the second note moved higher or lower.
  3. Pick up the guitar and choose a simple half-step bend.
  4. First play the target note one fret higher, then try to reach that pitch with a bend.
  5. Listen not only to bends, but also to normal fretted notes: do any of them go sharp because you are pressing too hard?

The goal is not to achieve a perfect trainer score. The point is to start hearing small deviations and recognising them in your own playing.

That is exactly why I built this trainer: to open it, listen for a few minutes, check myself, and return to the guitar with a more attentive ear.
